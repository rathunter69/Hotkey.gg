## C. How they teach

Concepts and sequence, not phrasing: what they explain before what, where they slow down, what they insist on, what they demonstrate versus tell. One paragraph per tier-A module; for accounting, DCF and LBO, one line per tier-A lesson underneath.

### Excel Crash Course

**excel M1.** Opens with a premise rather than a feature: analysts live in Excel, so the keyboard comes first, and learners are told to take the mouse away and accept early frustration. Then a short anatomy of the window across versions (Name Box, formula bar, sheets, tabs), the difference between tapped Alt sequences and held Ctrl chords, and a walk along the Ribbon saying which tools the course will lean on and which it will not. It closes with a one-time settings pass (calculation mode, iteration, Enter behaviour, animation, file protection), each change shown live with one sentence of why, before any drill begins.

**excel M2.** Teaches through one tiny running build: a few-line income grid typed by keyboard, rebuilt and formatted on a second sheet, then added together on a third. Each step introduces only what the grid needs next: widths, a pointed formula, copying across, the Format Cells dialog driven by keyboard, a house number format, blue inputs, cross-sheet pointing, pasting formats. The rhythm is tell, pause so the learner tries, then a slow demonstration with every key spoken aloud. The instructor insists the mouse stays untouched, repeats sequences until they stick, and says which chords deserve memorising and which are only worth recognising.

**excel M3.** The longest module keeps the same grid and layers editing and structure onto it in short drills: panes, in-cell editing and the edit-versus-point switch, clearing, fills, inserting rows and columns, then a long stop on Paste Special and its pitfalls. Naming is shown and then argued against; anchoring is taught by letting a fill fail first; grouping of rows and of sheets arrives with warnings. It then turns to reading a sheet: precedent keys before trace arrows, Go To Special for colouring inputs. The slowest passages are formula-driven conditional formats, built up from a deliberately trivial rule, and custom number codes, stripped bare and rebuilt piece by piece. Error codes and print setup close it.

**excel M4.** Starts with what a function is (name, brackets, arguments) using SUM and AVERAGE before any logic. IF is taught on a tiny sheet of everyday questions: words as outputs first, then numbers, then a nested version left deliberately awkward so AND and OR can replace it at the end of the module. IFS sits directly under the nested form on a small forecast. Error trapping and text joining share one lesson and are reused at once on the running practice statement, where the override pattern follows. Dates come in two passes: header functions first, take-apart functions later, shown as the clumsier route. Every formula is built live after a pause for the learner to try.

**excel M5.** Five retrieval functions are introduced together on a twelve-cell table with typed positions, only to show mechanics. The next lesson breaks them by inserting a row and a column, which sets up MATCH as the cure; one long exercise then rebuilds each function with MATCH off two drop-downs and re-tests by inserting. The instructor prefers OFFSET with MATCH, where our standard prefers INDEX. INDIRECT gets three lessons (text building, R1C1, ADDRESS), framed as things met in other people's files, and ROWS and COLUMNS counters remove the last typed numbers. Data tables are taught as strict layout rules, then the iteration workaround. XLOOKUP closes as a comparison, ending where INDEX wins.

**excel M6.** Opens with SUMPRODUCT on a small quantities-and-prices table, doing the long multiplication first so the saving is visible, then stops to explain how TRUE and FALSE become 1 and 0 before putting conditions inside the arrays. The same two-selector questions are answered again with SUMIF, AVERAGEIF and SUMIFS so the routes can be compared. The small functions (ABS, CEILING, FLOOR, the ROUND family, MIN, MAX, the COUNT variants) get a minute or two each on tiny input-and-answer cells, each pinned to one modeling use such as flooring a debt balance at zero. Time-value functions close the module: PV and FV by their arguments, NPV contrasted with PV, and the first-period gap between NPV and XNPV demonstrated live.

**excel M7.** Each text function is shown on one short word with its arguments in cells, changed live so the output moves: length, left, right, middle, case, then the two position finders, then the two replacers. The instructor says these only sink in through use and goes straight to a phrase-trimming exercise solved several ways, ending with a version that must cope with a middle word of any length, built in helper cells and then combined. Flash Fill, Text to Columns and Remove Duplicates follow as quick one-off cleaning tools, and VALUE and DATEVALUE close. Four of the six videos are missing; in the tool video that survives, nothing is said about working on a copy or about values versus live formulas.

**excel M8.** Built as a ladder on one small company table: sort, then a two-level sort, then subtotals on the sorted copy, then AutoFilter, all framed as the run-up to pivot tables. Each tool gets an illustration, a pause-and-try on the practice sheet, then the instructor's run. Pivots take two thirds of the module: the four areas and dragging fields, then Value Field Settings and Show Values As, then a second video that answers ten questions one after another by re-arranging a single pivot, including a misread question corrected on screen. The pivot is sold as the replacement for lookup formulas on ad-hoc questions; refreshing and reading a pivot from a page are not covered.

**excel M9.** Two capstone puzzles that need functions from different modules combined. The first is reasoned aloud: state the rule in words, reach for the obvious IF, then look for an arithmetic route that scales (a rounded month fraction acting as a switch inside EOMONTH) and test the rows around the boundary. The learner is sent back to solve it with IF as well, to feel why the compact form matters once halves become quarters. The second (video missing; read from the practice sheet) starts with a COUNTIF primer, then counts rows in which any of several names appear, by capped per-row flags and by COUNTIF inside SUMPRODUCT.

### Financial Statement Modeling

**fsm M2.** Opens with rules before any modeling. Formatting conventions come first (color code, number style, the currency-sign rule), presented as equal in weight to the numbers because every model is assumed to reach a client. Then a run of rules of thumb, each shown as a bad example followed by the fix on a live sheet: a rate buried in a formula, a typed subtotal, a formula nobody can audit, hidden rows, merged headers, chained links. Auditing keys are demonstrated inside that run rather than as a separate topic. The instructor takes firm positions (no named ranges, no linked files) and gives the reason each time, then closes on structure: sheet count, template versus one-off, period length.

**fsm M4.** Starts by touring the finished model so the learner sees the destination, then hands over an almost empty shell: labels, shaded input cells, a date row and a live balance check, nothing else. The learner is sent to enter history alone before the solution video, a pattern repeated all course. The solution slows down where judgment is needed (which reported lines to split, which to combine, where D&A hides) and hurries through the typing. Accounting is re-taught at the moment a line appears, deferred revenue and the equity lines especially, with small worked entries. Every block ends by re-adding against the source and reading the check; twice the instructor stops to ask what the statements say about the business.

**fsm M5.** Separates two ideas at the outset: the mechanics of a forecast and the substance of its assumptions, saying the second matters more. Assumptions are gathered in one block under the statement, seeded from an outside forecast for the early years and then carried flat, with the instructor saying which carried values worry him (growth) and which do not (margin). Lines that need schedules are left shaded and named as deferred, so the learner knows why they are empty. The build video narrates small wiring choices and their copy-error trade-offs. It ends with two outside sanity checks rather than an internal one. The reading restates each line's default driver and the average-versus-opening interest choice.

**fsm M6.** Concept before keys. A short framing video states the single idea (working-capital lines follow the operating line that creates them, with days held constant), then a fill-in exercise asks the learner to name a driver for each line before any formula is written; the solution admits more than one defensible answer and names revenue as the fallback. Catch-all lines get their own rule: operating, grow; financial or unknown, hold flat. Only then does the build begin, line by line, cash skipped until the cash flow exists. Split panes are taught mid-build, when the sheet gets long enough to need them. Fuller days-based schedules are promised for later, keeping the first pass simple.

**fsm M7.** Positions the cash flow statement as the last statement and as pure reconciliation: nothing is forecast on it and no history is entered. Learners unsure of the accounting are routed through three re-used accounting lessons first; the confident may skip them. The build is set as an exercise, then demonstrated with the sign rules stated flatly and the intuition given once, on receivables. The instructor's warnings are specific: never type a zero, check that a sum has not caught its own row, do not overwrite the cash balance with the period's change. A recap lesson closes the module, walking the model top to bottom and listing which lines remain open and which schedule will fill each.

**fsm M8.** Teaches the roll-forward as a shape before any numbers: four concept videos (the generic roll, fixed assets, retained earnings, when a line deserves a schedule), each under three minutes. The fixed-asset build then starts by deleting the pre-built answer and asking why this line cannot simply grow with revenue. History is laid beside the forecast only to frame the drivers. The instructor favors a simple ratio method and says openly that a detailed waterfall can fake precision, deferring it. The hardest step, backing out additions to a catch-all asset without double counting its amortization, is named as the hardest and the learner told to rewatch. Each schedule ends with a link back and a read of the trend.

**fsm M9.** The module opens with the revolver as an idea, not a formula: a drawn picture of the surplus and deficit cases, with minimum cash and the year's cash flow stacked up before any cell is touched. It then argues why a cash-rich case still needs the plug, builds it in two steps (needs analysis, then a one-line MIN roll-forward) and stress-tests it on screen. Interest follows, framed by backing rates out of history, and only then is circularity named: a two-cell toy, a deliberate blow-up, the loop traced through the statements, and the breaker. They slow down on the two classic mistakes and insist every circular line carries the switch.

**fsm M12.** One exercise, taught as a procedure rather than a list of tips. The learner is first told the single cause of any imbalance, then given a broken copy of the finished model and asked to try alone. The solution walks the balance sheet line by line, predicting each cash effect off to the side before looking, marking lines as verified and leaving cash to the end. Easy lines go fast; PP&E and retained earnings are slowed down because several movements net into one line. The hardest errors surface as unmarked leftovers. The close insists the method is guaranteed, and that a two-minute eyeball for wrong signs comes first.

**fsm M13.** Starts from the question of where a growth rate comes from, then rebuilds revenue from product lines. Three short exercises with solutions: lay out history and back into implied prices; bring in forecast units and prices for the near years and hold them flat after; then rewire the scenario block so cases act on volume and price rather than on growth. Data entry is done quickly; time goes on unit scale, sanity checks against known prices, and reading which driver explains the total. A deferred-revenue tangent is offered as optional. The last exercise is set as a struggle-first challenge, with the requirement stated as behaviour: toggling must flow through.

**fsm M15.** Working capital is revisited after the core model is done. Receivables and inventory each get a roll-forward schedule with a selector between a simple approach and an explicit days or turnover assumption (read from the workbook, as those videos are missing). The surviving lesson teaches the operating cycle with a small retailer example before touching the model, then computes days for inventory, receivables and payables on average balances, nets them into a cash conversion cycle and reads the sign. Liquidity ratios are shown rather than built, with a note that lumped balance-sheet lines must be unpicked first.

**fsm M16.** Depreciation is taught twice on purpose. The core model used a shortcut, depreciation as a share of capex drifting toward one, and this module replaces it with a waterfall: existing assets running off on one row, each forecast year's capex on its own row over a useful life backed out of the accounts. The introduction video is missing, so the structure was read from the workbook; the remaining clip recaps the shortcut, explains why the ratio should approach one for a mature business, and shows what breaks on the income statement when the old line is removed before the new schedule is linked.

### Accounting Crash Course

**accounting M1.** Opens with why a shared rulebook exists and who reads the numbers, then who writes the rules, and moves quickly through a list of assumptions and constraints. It slows down twice: for accrual, taught with one sale on four dates and two questions (when is it revenue, when is it a cost), and for historical cost, where it stops to defend a rule that plainly understates assets. Filings are told rather than practised; an annual filing is scrolled on screen to show where an analyst actually reads. The learner is told outright that the rule-makers can be skipped in favour of the statements.

| Lesson | Concept | Built from or contrasted with | Misconception headed off | Check or rule of thumb left |
| :- | :- | :- | :- | :- |
| 01-03 Overview - Why is Accounting Important | Accounting as a common measuring language | Three kinds of reader (managers, investors, lenders), each facing a decision that needs two companies compared on the same basis | That the rules only matter for listed companies; sizeable private firms follow the same ones | Two sets of figures are comparable only when produced under one rulebook |
| 01-04 Basic Accounting Principles | Accrual (earning and matching) and historical cost | One sale followed across four dates, contrasted with simply counting cash in and cash out; a plot of land held at its old price | Revenue is cash received and cost is cash paid; a balance sheet shows what things are worth | Revenue and its cost both land on the delivery date; when unsure, do not overstate assets or income |
| 01-05 Financial Reportings & Important Filings | Periodic filings of a listed company | Annual and quarterly reports contrasted on detail and on audit | The glossy annual report is the filing; quarterly numbers are audited | Pull data from the regulator's copy; annual is audited, quarterly only reviewed |
| 01-06 Reading the 10K | The map of an annual filing | Scrolling a real filing part by part and naming which items an analyst reads | That it has to be read front to back | Business description, selected data, management discussion, statements, notes; the basic share count is on the cover |

**accounting M2.** Gives the whole income statement as a map, then goes down it one line per lesson. Each line gets the same treatment: a definition, published statements and footnote extracts from familiar companies to show the line in the wild, then a pause-and-answer sorting quiz with a decoy. Costs are sorted first by function, then by timing, which is where depreciation enters as matching applied to a long-lived purchase. It insists throughout that an expense is not a payment and keeps promising the cash flow statement. Analysts' profit measures wait until every line is known. It ends with a build: a small business's first year on a blank template, solved live in a spreadsheet, then extended.

| 02-16 The small-business build, Part 2 | Non-operating events and the share count | Three further events posted onto the statement just built | New shares are simply added to the count; a settlement or bank interest belongs in revenue | Operating profit and EBITDA are unchanged by all three; shares are weighted by time outstanding |

**accounting M3.** Sets the balance sheet against the income statement (a date versus a period), lists assets and then funding, each with a test for what qualifies and a sorting quiz. It returns to the small business from the previous module and explains balancing as the two sides of every event, source and use, before attaching the bookkeepers' words and T-accounts, which it openly plays down. The core is a slow walk down the finished income statement, naming for every line the balance-sheet account it moves and the matching move in retained earnings. Opening balances plus net movements give the closing sheet, proved with a zero check. Presentation order comes last.

| 03-02 An Introduction to Liabilities | Liabilities and equity as sources of funds | The other half of the equation: what is owed and what the owners have put in or left in; a sorting quiz | Any risk or any expense is a liability | A liability is measurable and probable; equity grows by capital paid in and by profit kept |
| 03-03 Double Entry Accounting | Double entry | Day one of the small business: money in from a lender and the owner becomes cash, then cash is swapped for stock and equipment; source and use first, the bookkeepers' words after | Every event changes both sides of the balance sheet; credit means an increase | Total debits equal total credits; debit is a use, credit is a source |

**accounting M4.** Works down the asset side in balance-sheet order. Every line gets the same treatment: a plain definition, a look at how a large listed company words it in its notes, a two-sided entry showing which balances move, then a pause-and-try question. The slow stretches are stock costing (a third of the module) and depreciation with purchases in successive years, both worked number by number. The instructor insists that nothing on the asset side is ever marked up, only down, and comes back to that rule for stock, equipment and goodwill. PP&E, intangibles and goodwill each close on the same opening-to-closing bridge picture.

| :- | :- | :- | :- | :- |
| 04-01 Cash, Receivables & Prepaid Expenses | Receivables and prepaid expenses as timing assets | The accrual rule applied twice: a sale earned before the cash arrives, and cash paid before the service is used; each shown as a two-sided entry that reaches the income statement through retained earnings. | That a line with 'expenses' in its name is a cost on the income statement; a prepayment is an asset until it is used. | Once the prepaid period is over the asset is zero and the whole amount has passed through cost. |
| 04-02 Inventory | Inventory and how its cost reaches the income statement | A balance roll (opening, purchases, cost of sales, closing), then the same units costed three ways while purchase prices climb. | That the costing method tracks which physical item was sold; it only assigns values. | Revenue is the same under every method, and cost of sales plus closing stock always equals opening stock plus purchases. |
| 04-03 Property, Plant & Equipment, Part 1 | Net PP&E and accumulated depreciation | One machine depreciated evenly, then a small business adding new equipment at each year-end. | That the year's charge and the accumulated balance are one number, or that an asset bought on the last day depreciates in that year. | Net equals gross less accumulated at every date; an asset past its life adds nothing more to the charge. |
| 04-04 Property, Plant & Equipment, Part 2 | Write-offs, asset sales and the PP&E bridge | A contrast with planned depreciation; a sale entry in which cash, book value and gain have to balance; then a four-step bridge. | That the sale price comes off PP&E; only book value does, and the gain is not cash. | Closing PP&E = opening + capex - depreciation - book value of disposals. |

**accounting M5.** Liabilities first, then a five-lesson detour into leases, then equity, then a half-hour capstone. Payables, accruals and deferred revenue are each taught as the gap between what was booked and what was paid, using the running small-business case. Leases are introduced as debt in disguise; the instructor admits the entries are unintuitive and leans on them anyway: day-one measurement by discounting, then both treatments on one example, then a real balance sheet. Equity goes line by line and ends on a buyback puzzle. The capstone deliberately derives cash the slow way so that the next module's statement feels earned.

**accounting M6.** Starts from the problem the capstone left behind: cash was hard to find. The statement is justified before it is built (profit needs judgment, cash does not). Operating cash is then taught as a ladder of tiny cases, each answered by intuition first and rebuilt from net income second, so signs are reasoned, not memorised. One rule is repeated throughout: an asset going up uses cash, a liability going up supplies it. Investing and financing get a short tour on a published statement. The close maps balance-sheet regions to the three sections, then has the learner wire the statement from the capstone's numbers and tie it to the earlier hand count.

| Lesson | Concept | Built from or contrasted with | Misconception headed off | Check or rule of thumb left |
| :- | :- | :- | :- | :- |
| 06-01 The Cash Flow Statement | What the cash flow statement is for | The pain of deriving cash by hand in the capstone; two opposite cases (profit without cash, cash without profit). | That either the income statement or the cash flow statement can be read alone. | Net income, plus non-cash items, plus or minus working-capital changes, equals operating cash. |
| 06-02 Cash From Operations | Cash from operations by the indirect method | A ladder from an all-cash income statement up to credit sales, stock bought partly on credit, prepaid cards and unpaid wages. | That a rise in receivables or stock is good for cash because assets went up. | The indirect total has to equal the plain count of cash in less cash out. |
| 06-03 Cash From Financing & Cash From Investing | Investing, financing and the statement as a whole | Balance sheet regions mapped to sections: earnings and current items, long-term assets, debt and equity. | That the cash flow statement holds information the other two statements do not. | The three sections add up to the change in cash; opening plus change equals the balance sheet's closing cash. |
| 06-04 The small-business build, Part 4 | Building the cash flow statement from the other two | The capstone's income statement and two balance sheets, referenced by formula one line at a time. | That scrapping an asset produces an investing cash flow, or that the write-off reduced cash. | Net change equals the hand-derived figure; after that the balance sheet's cash reads the statement. |

**accounting M7.** One long lesson that treats ratios as the payoff for knowing how the statements link. Four families are named up front and then taken in turn: activity, liquidity, profitability and returns, leverage. Each ratio is defined, given a direction (higher or lower is better) and then straight away qualified with a case where the rule of thumb misleads: a tight operator with a low current ratio, idle cash dragging down return on assets, leverage flattering one of two identical firms. ### Trading Comps

**trading-comps M1.** Starts from intuition, not formulas: an everyday asset frames equity against whole-asset value, book against market, and relative against cash-flow valuation. A tiny balance sheet then defines enterprise value from the asset side, and the learner solves the bridge before it is shown. Only then do multiples arrive, each on the same card: definition, purpose, flaws, where it fits, what drives it. Leverage and accounting noise are the recurring tests. A one-page worked example shows that applying a multiple is trivial and the labour is in the spreading. The module closes on skepticism: a question-and-answer round on what comps cannot tell you.

**trading-comps M2.** Shows the destination first (a finished output page and a range summary), then the process, then a tour of the template, and only then data entry. Peer selection is demonstrated as triangulation with a written verdict per candidate, not told as rules. The spread itself is slow and procedural: source open beside the model, one block at a time, units and signs converted on entry, each subtotal tied to the source before moving on, each input noted with its origin. LTM and calendarization are both drawn as timelines before any formula appears. The instructor spreads one period, then hands its twin to the learner. Insisted on throughout: paper trail, ties, no double counting.

**trading-comps M5.** All data entry is finished before any output is built, then the course stops for a short Excel interlude: names and anchoring are explained on a slide, and the two-way lookup is built live, one argument at a time, reading the function prompts aloud. Each output tab follows one rhythm: a slide of the finished table with the reason for every column, a hand-off to the learner, then a review that reads the numbers rather than the formulas. The review starts from the target, compares each peer on size, leverage, growth and margin, and holds the outlier question open until the multiples confirm it. The P/E variant is posed as a question one lesson before it is answered.

### Transaction Comps

**transaction-comps M2.** A short method statement comes first, framed as the same steps as trading comps, then a deliberately tiny round-number exercise is solved live before any real data appears. The solution is used to make two points they insist on: enterprise multiples need the net debt bridge, and different multiples can give opposite verdicts on one target. Premiums and synergies get a brief slide-only lesson as the two ideas unique to deals. The remainder slows right down on sourcing: where deals come from, how to screen, which document holds which number, with repeated warnings that vendor data is a finder and never the source.

### Course materials, second pass (2026-09-30): exercise files and shortcut sheets

Wolf asked whether the shortcut sheets and the sample workbooks had been audited for how an exercise is shelled. The workbooks had been parsed for what each exercise asks (the *(exercise)* rows in A), not for how the files are built; the shortcut sheets had been skipped, as the brief said. Both were done in this pass: the five shortcut sheets read against screenplay section 10, and 75 sheets in 12 exercise workbooks plus the 14 numbered pairs of model-step files profiled for structure (what is prefilled, what is blank, how the blank cells are marked, how a build is staged). Structure only: counts of cells by kind, no label, figure or layout.

**How their exercise files are built.**

- **One tab per topic, with its answer beside it.** The Excel practice book holds 38 tabs: a topic tab and, next to it, a twin with the same name and an answer suffix (14 pairs); the other ten tabs are demonstration sheets with nothing to fill. The accounting book pairs an empty and a done tab the same way; the small valuation exercises are two-tab files (exercise, solution). The exam book names its tabs by question range, each with an answer twin.
- **The start state is the finished sheet minus the answers.** In every pair the two tabs are identical except for the answer cells: all labels, headings, inputs, formats and column widths are already there. Almost nothing else differs (two stray cells in the whole practice book).
- **Small tasks.** A function exercise is 3 to 18 answer cells (median about 7; two table exercises run past 40 because a data table or a pivot fills them) in one to five contiguous blocks, on a tab of 5 to 40 rows by 5 to 11 columns, and nearly every answer cell is a formula. Where the skill needs bulk (counting, conditional formats, printing, text to columns) the tab carries a list of 800 to 7,000 rows and still only three or four answer cells, placed at the top beside the list.
- **The question sits on the sheet.** A bold question header, a short block of labelled inputs, then a two-column table with the question in words on the left and the empty answer cell on the right; a longer tab stacks several numbered questions down the page, each with its own inputs. Hints are notes in the next column with an arrow pointing back at the cell.
- **How an answer cell is marked.** In the practice book about two answer cells in five are an empty bordered box, a third carry a pale fill (yellow for single answers, grey for the body of a table) and the rest are unmarked; the exam and the valuation exercises shade every answer cell in one colour; the model steps mark almost nothing. Inputs are blue type throughout, often on a pale yellow fill, so blue-on-yellow means "given" and an empty box means "yours".
- **A reference block above the exercise.** Several tabs open with a worked table (function, a live formula, its inputs) and put the exercise under it on the same tab, so the syntax is in view while the learner works.
- **A full model is a chain of file pairs.** The LBO build ships as numbered steps, each an empty and a done file (fourteen steps in tier A). Each empty file is the previous done file plus the next block's labels, headers and a few seed formulas (checked on two consecutive steps: 45 and 26 cells added, one changed). The learner fills 15 to about 200 cells a step (median about 70), almost all formulas, in two to eight blocks, and every step sits lower on the same single sheet: the model grows downward, one block per step. The precedent-transactions file is the same idea in one pair: about 215 cells to fill, three quarters of them typed from documents and the rest formulas, with the two kinds in different fills.
- **Checking is by eye.** No tab checks itself: the learner compares with the twin. Format-only exercises (conditional formatting) differ from their answers only in the rules applied, so there is nothing to compare but the look.
- **House habits.** No protected sheets and no frozen panes anywhere; gridlines left on in most files (off on 15 of the 75 exercise sheets, on throughout the model steps); drop-downs rare (one to three on a handful of tabs, for a case or a lookup selector); almost no defined names (one valuation exercise is the exception); cell notes used heavily in the finished model (about a hundred) to explain individual cells.

**What to take for our own shells, and what not.** Take the cut: solve first, then empty only the graded cells (build note in H). Take the chain for Chapters 5 and 6: a lesson starts where the last one ended, plus the next block's labels, on one growing sheet. Take the size discipline for drills, and the long-list-few-answers shape for data lessons. Do not take the on-sheet question, the answer boxes or the hint arrows: our sheets are Clearcoat work products and the goal panel, teach line and pulse do that job, which also keeps the pages looking like something a desk would send. Do not take checking by eye: grading the end state is the point of the engine. Their files mostly leave gridlines on; ours come off as a taught habit in Chapters 1 and 2, and nothing here argues for changing that.

**Their shortcut sheets against our keys syllabus.** Two sheets matter (the current Windows and Mac sheet, and the one for the subscription version, which is the same list with comments added); the two older sheets are mostly a list of the course's own add-in chords. Of about ninety native keys on the current sheets, every one is already in a script or in screenplay section 10 except six, and five of those were already in this checklist from the lessons: open a file (G001, Reference), the three Clear commands (G008, G016), and split panes (section A, mapped to 5.4.1). The sixth is a settings box, held as G196. Nothing on the sheets contradicts a key we teach. Two things worth having from them:

- **The add-in explains the "cycle".** The older sheets show the course's add-in taking over Ctrl+Shift+1, Ctrl+Shift+4 and Ctrl+Shift+5 as number, currency and percent cycles, and say that add-ins can overwrite native keys. That is where the idea that Ctrl+Shift+1 cycles comes from, and it is the same on a desk with a commercial add-in. One Reference line says so (G195).
- **Dialog keys.** Their list for moving inside a dialog is Tab, Ctrl+Tab between tabs, Alt with the underlined letter, arrows in a list, Space for a tick box: all true, all already in 10.1. It does not mention that the row of tabs answers to first letters, which is what our 1.5.3 uses and what the desktop test confirmed.

Not opened in this pass either: the Mac settings sheet (its content is repeated in the footer of the current sheet, and matches our Mac notes), the PowerPoint shortcuts kit, the LAMBDA workbook, and everything the brief rules out (filings, third-party research, the tier-C deal files).
